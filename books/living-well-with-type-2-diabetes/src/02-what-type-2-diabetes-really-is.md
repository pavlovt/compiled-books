# Chapter 1. What Type 2 Diabetes Really Is

## The short version

Type 2 diabetes is a condition in which there is too much sugar (glucose) in the blood, most of the time. It happens because two things go wrong at once:

1.  **Insulin resistance.** Your muscles, liver and fat tissue stop responding well to insulin, the hormone that moves glucose out of the blood and into cells.
2.  **Failing insulin production.** The beta cells in your pancreas try to compensate by making more insulin. Over years, they become exhausted and produce less than your body needs.

When insulin cannot do its job, glucose builds up in the blood. High blood glucose is not painful in the short term, which is why many people ignore it. Over years, however, it damages blood vessels and nerves throughout the body.

## Why it develops

Type 2 diabetes is not caused by eating sugar, and it is not a personal failure. It is the result of several factors working together:

- **Genes.** A family history of type 2 diabetes raises your risk a lot. Some people develop it at a normal weight because of their genes.
- **Fat in the wrong places.** Fat stored inside the abdomen (visceral fat) and inside the liver and pancreas causes far more harm than fat under the skin. A waist that grows over the years is often the first sign.
- **Age.** Muscle mass falls and insulin resistance rises with age, especially after 40.
- **Inactivity.** Muscles are the main place where the body stores and uses glucose. Unused muscles take up less of it.
- **Poor sleep and chronic stress,** which raise stress hormones and appetite.
- **Some medicines,** such as steroids, which raise blood sugar.

## The “twin cycle”: why weight matters so much

Research led by Professor Roy Taylor at Newcastle University explains why type 2 diabetes is so closely linked to weight. When the body takes in more energy than it uses over many years, the extra fat spills into the liver. A fatty liver becomes resistant to insulin and keeps releasing glucose into the blood, even at night and between meals.

The liver then exports fat to the pancreas. Fat in the pancreas disrupts the beta cells that make insulin. The result is a vicious circle: a fatty liver drives blood sugar up, and a fatty pancreas cannot produce enough insulin to bring it down.

The good news is that this cycle can run in reverse. When people lose enough weight, fat leaves the liver first, within weeks, and then the pancreas. In many people, insulin production partly recovers. This is the basis of diabetes remission, covered in Chapter 3.

## It is progressive, but the speed is not fixed

Type 2 diabetes is usually described as a progressive disease. Without changes, the pancreas tends to produce less insulin each year, and people need more medicine over time. But the rate of decline is not fixed. Weight loss, physical activity and good blood sugar control early in the disease slow it down, and sometimes stop or reverse it for years.

There is also a “legacy effect.” Large long-term studies, such as the UK Prospective Diabetes Study, found that people who had good blood sugar control in the first years after diagnosis had fewer heart attacks and complications decades later, even after their control was no longer different from others. In other words, effort now pays off for a very long time.

## What high blood sugar does over time

Glucose in high amounts sticks to proteins in the blood and vessel walls and damages them. Over years this leads to two groups of complications:

**Small blood vessel damage (microvascular):**

- **Eyes (retinopathy).** Damage to the small vessels at the back of the eye. It is a leading cause of sight loss in working-age adults, but it is preventable and treatable if caught early.
- **Kidneys (nephropathy).** The kidneys’ filters slowly leak protein and lose function.
- **Nerves (neuropathy).** Numbness, tingling or pain, usually starting in the feet. It can also affect digestion, the bladder and sexual function.

**Large blood vessel damage (macrovascular):**

- **Heart attack and heart failure.**
- **Stroke.**
- **Peripheral artery disease,** with poor circulation in the legs.

Heart and blood vessel disease is the leading cause of death in people with type 2 diabetes. This is why modern diabetes care is not only about blood sugar. Blood pressure, cholesterol, kidney protection and not smoking matter just as much. Chapter 10 covers this in detail.

## What you can control

Most of the risk from type 2 diabetes is within your influence. Studies such as the Steno-2 trial showed that treating several risk factors together (blood sugar, blood pressure, cholesterol, lifestyle, and aspirin where appropriate) cut the risk of heart attack, stroke and death roughly in half over many years.

The levers, in rough order of impact, are:

1.  **Losing weight if you carry excess weight, especially around the waist.**
2.  **Not smoking.**
3.  **Keeping blood pressure and cholesterol at target,** usually with medicine as well as lifestyle.
4.  **Keeping blood sugar near target** without frequent low blood sugar.
5.  **Being physically active every day,** with strength training 2–3 times a week.
6.  **Eating a diet built on vegetables, legumes, whole grains, fish, nuts and olive oil,** with fewer refined carbohydrates.
7.  **Sleeping enough and managing stress.**
8.  **Going to your check-ups:** eyes, kidneys, feet, teeth.

> **What to do**
>
> - Accept that type 2 diabetes is a long-term condition, but not a fixed fate. Early effort has the largest and longest-lasting payoff.
> - Measure your waist today, at the level of your navel, and write it down. You will track it throughout this book.
> - Think of diabetes care as protecting your heart, kidneys, eyes and nerves, not just lowering a sugar number.
