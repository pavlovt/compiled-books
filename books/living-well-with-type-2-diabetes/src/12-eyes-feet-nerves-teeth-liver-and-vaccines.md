# Chapter 11. Eyes, Feet, Nerves, Teeth, Liver and Vaccines

## Eyes

Diabetic retinopathy is damage to the small blood vessels of the retina at the back of the eye. In its early stages it causes no symptoms at all. By the time vision blurs, damage may be advanced. Yet with regular screening and modern treatment, such as laser and injections, severe sight loss is largely preventable.

**What to do:**

- **Get a dilated eye examination or retinal photograph** at diagnosis and then every 1–2 years if normal, or more often if any changes are found.
- **Control blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol.** All three slow retinopathy.
- **Note:** very rapid improvement in blood sugar, for example after starting a powerful new medicine, can occasionally cause temporary worsening of existing retinopathy. If you have known retinopathy, tell your eye doctor when major treatment changes are planned.
- **See an eye doctor promptly** if you notice blurred vision, dark spots, floaters, flashes of light, or a shadow in your vision.

People with diabetes also have higher rates of cataract and glaucoma, which are checked at the same examination.

## Feet

Foot problems are among the most serious and most preventable complications of diabetes. Two processes combine: **nerve damage**, which means you may not feel an injury, and **poor circulation**, which means wounds heal slowly. A small blister or cut can become an ulcer, and an infected ulcer can lead to amputation. Most amputations in diabetes start with a foot ulcer, and most ulcers are preventable.

### Daily foot care

1.  **Look at your feet every day,** including between the toes and the soles. Use a mirror or ask someone to help. Look for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling, hard skin, and changes in colour.
2.  **Wash your feet daily** in warm (not hot) water, and dry carefully, especially between the toes. Test the water temperature with your elbow, not your foot.
3.  **Moisturise dry skin,** but not between the toes.
4.  **Cut toenails straight across** and file sharp edges. If you have nerve damage, poor vision or thick nails, see a podiatrist.
5.  **Never walk barefoot,** even at home.
6.  **Wear well-fitting shoes** with enough room for your toes, and check inside them for stones or rough seams before putting them on.
7.  **Wear clean, seamless socks** and change them daily, or more often if your feet sweat. Damp or dirty socks feed athlete’s foot and nail fungus, which crack the skin and let bacteria in. Choose cotton, wool or moisture-wicking blends rather than nylon, and avoid tight elastic tops that leave marks; “diabetic socks” have loose tops and flat seams. Light-coloured socks show blood or discharge from a wound you cannot feel. Do not wear darned socks or socks with holes, which create pressure points. Dry well between your toes before putting socks on.
8.  **Do not treat corns or calluses yourself** with blades or corn plasters. See a podiatrist.
9.  **Avoid heating pads and hot water bottles** on your feet.

### Annual foot examination

Your doctor or nurse should check your feet at least once a year, testing sensation with a fine nylon filament (monofilament) and checking the pulses. If you have nerve damage, deformity or a past ulcer, checks should be more frequent.

### When to seek help the same day

- A cut, blister or sore that does not start healing within 1–2 days.
- Redness, warmth, swelling or pus.
- A change in the shape or colour of the foot.
- Pain or throbbing in the foot, or a foot that becomes hot and swollen without injury.

## Nerves (neuropathy)

Long-term high blood sugar can damage nerves.

- **Peripheral neuropathy** usually starts in the toes and feet: numbness, tingling, burning or shooting pains, often worse at night. Loss of feeling is the dangerous part, because injuries go unnoticed.
- **Autonomic neuropathy** affects the nerves that control internal organs: dizziness when standing, a fast resting heart rate, slow stomach emptying (bloating, nausea, unpredictable blood sugars), bladder problems, and sexual problems.

**What helps:** good blood sugar control slows progression. Painful neuropathy can be treated with medicines such as duloxetine or pregabalin. Check vitamin B12 if you take metformin, because B12 deficiency can cause or worsen neuropathy.

### Sexual health

Erectile dysfunction is common in men with diabetes because of nerve and blood vessel damage. It can also be an early warning sign of heart disease. It is very treatable, so mention it to your doctor; there is no need for embarrassment. Women with diabetes may experience vaginal dryness and more frequent infections, which are also treatable.

## Teeth and gums

People with diabetes are more prone to gum disease (periodontitis), and gum disease in turn makes blood sugar harder to control. Treating gum disease can lower HbA1c slightly.

- Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, and clean between teeth daily with floss or interdental brushes.
- See a dentist at least once a year, or every 6 months if you have gum problems.
- Tell your dentist you have diabetes and which medicines you take.
- Report bleeding gums, loose teeth or persistent bad breath.

## Liver

Fatty liver disease, now called **metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)**, affects more than half of people with type 2 diabetes. In some people it progresses to inflammation and scarring (fibrosis), and eventually to cirrhosis or liver cancer.

- **Ask your doctor to calculate your FIB-4 score** from routine blood tests. If it is raised, a liver scan such as elastography (FibroScan) can measure scarring.
- **Weight loss is the main treatment.** Losing 5% of body weight reduces liver fat; 7–10% can reduce inflammation and even scarring.
- **Limit alcohol** strictly if you have fatty liver.
- **Some diabetes medicines help,** including GLP-1 receptor agonists and pioglitazone.
- **Coffee** appears to be protective for the liver.

## Vaccines

Infections hit people with diabetes harder, and illness disrupts blood sugar. Ask your doctor about:

- **Influenza** every autumn.
- **Pneumococcal** vaccine against pneumonia.
- **COVID-19** boosters as recommended.
- **Shingles (herpes zoster)** for adults aged 50 and over.
- **Hepatitis B** for adults with diabetes who are not already immune, especially those under 60.
- **Tetanus-diphtheria** boosters every 10 years.
- **RSV** for older adults and those at increased risk, according to national guidance.

Recommendations vary by country and change over time, so check with your doctor which ones apply to you in Bulgaria.

## Other things to know

- **Hearing:** hearing loss is more common in diabetes. Get a hearing check if you notice problems.
- **Bones:** diabetes increases fracture risk. Strength training, enough protein, calcium and vitamin D, and preventing falls all help.
- **Mood and memory:** good diabetes control, exercise and blood pressure control protect the brain.
- **Cancer screening:** type 2 diabetes is linked to higher risk of some cancers, including colorectal and liver cancer. Keep up with recommended screening, such as bowel cancer screening from age 50.

> **What to do**
>
> - Have an eye examination at least every 1–2 years.
> - Look at your feet every day, and have them examined by a professional every year.
> - See a dentist at least once a year.
> - Ask for your FIB-4 liver score.
> - Ask which vaccines you need, and get the flu vaccine every autumn.
> - Mention numbness, tingling, dizziness on standing or sexual problems to your doctor.
