# Chapter 4. Eating Well with Type 2 Diabetes

## The goal of eating well

A good diet for type 2 diabetes does four things: it keeps blood sugar spikes small, it helps you reach and keep a healthy weight, it protects your heart, and it is enjoyable enough to keep for life. No food is completely forbidden. What matters is the overall pattern.

The pattern with the strongest evidence is the **Mediterranean diet**: lots of vegetables, legumes, fruit, whole grains, nuts and olive oil; fish and poultry; moderate dairy, mainly yogurt and cheese; little red and processed meat; few sweets. In the PREDIMED trial in Spain, a Mediterranean diet with extra olive oil or nuts reduced heart attacks and strokes by about 30% in people at high risk, many of whom had diabetes. Traditional Bulgarian food, with its vegetables, beans, yogurt and white cheese, fits this pattern well, if pastries, white bread and fatty meats are kept small.

## The plate method

The simplest way to build a meal is the plate method. Use a standard dinner plate, about 23 cm across:

- **Half the plate:** non-starchy vegetables. Salad, tomatoes, cucumber, peppers, cabbage, spinach, broccoli, courgettes, green beans, mushrooms, aubergine.
- **A quarter:** protein. Fish, chicken, turkey, eggs, lean meat, white cheese (sirene) in moderation, tofu, or beans and lentils.
- **A quarter:** carbohydrate foods. Whole-grain bread, bulgur, brown rice, potatoes, wholemeal pasta, beans, or fruit.
- **Add:** a little healthy fat, such as a tablespoon of olive oil on the salad or a few nuts.
- **Drink:** water, mineral water, unsweetened tea or coffee.

## Understanding carbohydrates

Carbohydrates have the biggest effect on blood sugar. They are not bad, but the type and amount matter.

**Better choices** are digested slowly and come with fibre:

- Legumes: lentils, beans, chickpeas, peas.
- Whole grains: oats, bulgur, barley, buckwheat, rye and wholemeal bread.
- Vegetables, including some starchy ones in moderate portions.
- Whole fruit, especially berries, apples, pears, citrus and kiwi.
- Plain yogurt and kefir.

**Foods to limit** are digested quickly and cause sharp spikes:

- Sugary drinks and fruit juice, including “100% natural” juice.
- White bread, white rolls and pastries such as banitsa, croissants and “mekitsi”.
- Sweets, cakes, biscuits, wafers and chocolate spreads.
- Large portions of white rice, white pasta and mashed potatoes.
- Dried fruit and very sweet fruits in large amounts (grapes, persimmons, figs, ripe bananas).
- Breakfast cereals with added sugar.

You do not have to count carbohydrates in grams unless you take mealtime insulin. A consistent portion, about a quarter of the plate or the size of your fist, works well for most people. If you check your blood sugar, you will learn which foods spike your own levels most.

## Fibre: the most underrated nutrient

Most adults eat about 15–20 g of fibre a day. People with diabetes benefit from at least 25–35 g a day. A large review commissioned by the World Health Organization found that people eating the most fibre had about 15–30% lower rates of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and early death than those eating the least.

Fibre helps in several ways. It slows the absorption of sugar, keeps you full, lowers LDL cholesterol, and feeds the gut bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids, which improve appetite control and inflammation.

Easy ways to reach 30 g:

- A bowl of oats for breakfast: about 4 g.
- A cup of cooked lentils or beans: about 13–15 g.
- Two slices of wholemeal or rye bread: about 5 g.
- An apple or pear with the skin: about 4–5 g.
- A large salad and a portion of cooked vegetables: about 6–8 g.
- A tablespoon of ground flaxseed: about 3 g.
- A handful of almonds or walnuts: about 2–3 g.

Increase fibre gradually over 2–3 weeks and drink more water, or you may feel bloated.

## Protein

Protein has little effect on blood sugar and helps you feel full. During weight loss, it protects your muscles, and after age 50 muscle loss accelerates. A reasonable goal for most people with normal kidney function is 1.0–1.2 g of protein per kg of body weight per day, spread across meals. If you have kidney disease, ask your doctor about the right amount.

Good sources: fish (especially oily fish such as mackerel, sardines and salmon, twice a week), chicken and turkey, eggs, plain yogurt and kefir, cottage cheese, white cheese in moderation, beans, lentils, chickpeas and tofu. Limit processed meats such as sausages, lukanka, salami and ham; they are linked to heart disease and contain a lot of salt.

## Fats

The type of fat matters more than the total amount.

- **Eat more:** extra virgin olive oil, nuts, seeds, avocado and oily fish.
- **Eat less:** butter, lard, fatty meats, processed meats, palm oil, and cream-based sauces.
- **Avoid:** industrial trans fats in some margarines, fried fast food and packaged baked goods.

## Salt

Too much salt raises blood pressure. Aim for less than 5 g of salt a day (about one teaspoon in total, including salt already in food). Most salt comes from bread, cheese, processed meats, pickles, ready meals and restaurant food rather than from the salt shaker. Brined white cheese and pickled vegetables (turshia) are very salty, so keep portions small.

## Timing and order of food

Two simple tricks reduce blood sugar spikes without changing what you eat:

1.  **Eat your vegetables and protein first, and carbohydrates last.** In small studies of people with type 2 diabetes, eating carbohydrates last, rather than first, cut the post-meal glucose rise by roughly half. The vegetables and protein slow stomach emptying and trigger gut hormones such as GLP-1 before the sugar arrives.
2.  **Eat more earlier in the day.** The body handles glucose better in the morning than in the evening. A bigger breakfast and lunch and a smaller, earlier dinner tend to give better blood sugar control. Aim to finish dinner 3–4 hours before bed.

> **If you take Xigduo or another SGLT2 inhibitor**, do not try strict time-restricted eating, such as eating only between 8 a.m. and 2 p.m., without your doctor’s advice. Take your tablets with meals, and avoid long periods without food. The safer goal is an earlier, lighter dinner.

## Drinks

- **Water** is the best drink. Mineral water is fine.
- **Coffee and tea,** without sugar, are fine and may even be protective. Green tea is a good choice.
- **Diet soft drinks** are better than sugary ones, but water is better still.
- **Fruit juice** acts almost like a sugary drink. Eat the whole fruit instead.
- **Alcohol** is covered in Chapter 5.

> **What to do**
>
> - Use the plate method for lunch and dinner: half vegetables, a quarter protein, a quarter carbohydrates.
> - Replace white bread with wholemeal or rye bread, and white rice with bulgur, lentils or buckwheat.
> - Eat legumes at least 3–4 times a week.
> - Eat vegetables and protein first, carbohydrates last.
> - Stop drinking juice and sugary drinks completely.
