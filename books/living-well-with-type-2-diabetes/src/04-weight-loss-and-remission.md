# Chapter 3. Weight Loss and Remission

## The single most powerful lever

For people with type 2 diabetes who carry excess weight, losing weight is the most powerful treatment available. It improves blood sugar, blood pressure, cholesterol, fatty liver, sleep apnoea, joint pain and mood, all at once. No single medicine does all of that.

The benefits grow with the amount lost:

| Weight lost | Typical benefit |
|------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| 5% of body weight | Better blood sugar, blood pressure and triglycerides; often less medicine needed |
| 10% | Large improvement in fatty liver; clear drop in HbA1c; better sleep apnoea |
| 15% or more | Remission becomes likely, especially within the first 6 years after diagnosis |

For a man weighing 100 kg, 5% is 5 kg, 10% is 10 kg and 15% is 15 kg.

## What remission means

Remission means an HbA1c below 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) for at least 3 months without any glucose-lowering medicine. It is called remission, not cure, because diabetes can return if weight comes back.

The best-known evidence comes from the **DiRECT trial** in the UK, published in 2017–2019. Adults who had been diagnosed within the previous 6 years followed a structured programme: about 12 weeks of a low-calorie formula diet (around 825–853 kcal a day), then gradual reintroduction of food, then long-term support.

- After 1 year, **46%** of people in the programme were in remission, compared with **4%** in the usual-care group.
- Among people who lost **15 kg or more**, **86%** were in remission.
- After 2 years, 36% were still in remission. Those who kept the weight off stayed in remission; those who regained it tended to relapse.

Remission is most likely if you are within a few years of diagnosis and do not yet need insulin. It is still possible later, but less likely, because fewer working beta cells remain. Even when remission does not happen, the same weight loss usually means better control on less medicine.

> **Safety first.** Very low-calorie diets must be done under medical supervision when you take diabetes or blood pressure medicine. Doses often need to be cut or stopped on day one. This is especially true for SGLT2 inhibitors such as dapagliflozin (in Xigduo), because a sharp fall in food and carbohydrate intake raises the risk of ketoacidosis. Never start a very low-calorie or very low-carbohydrate diet on your own while on these medicines.

## How to lose weight in practice

There is no single best diet. Low-fat, low-carbohydrate, Mediterranean and meal-replacement diets all work when people stick to them. The best diet is the one you can keep up. The common principles are:

1.  **Eat fewer calories than you use, steadily.** A deficit of 500–600 kcal a day usually gives 0.5–1 kg loss a week at first.
2.  **Eat more protein.** Protein keeps you full longer and protects muscle during weight loss. Aim for a palm-sized portion of protein at each meal: fish, chicken, eggs, yogurt, cottage cheese, beans or lentils.
3.  **Fill half your plate with vegetables.** They add volume with very few calories.
4.  **Cut liquid calories.** Sugary drinks, juice, alcohol and sweetened coffees are the easiest calories to remove.
5.  **Reduce refined carbohydrates:** white bread, pastries such as banitsa and croissants, sweets, white rice in large portions.
6.  **Keep regular meals.** Erratic eating leads to overeating later in the day.
7.  **Build in movement.** Exercise alone rarely causes large weight loss, but it is the best predictor of keeping weight off.

## Structured options to discuss with your doctor

- **Meal-replacement programmes.** Formula shakes or soups replacing some or all meals for a period, followed by careful food reintroduction. This is the DiRECT approach. It is very effective but needs supervision and medicine adjustment.
- **Weight-loss medicines.** GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide, and the combined GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide, lower blood sugar and typically produce about 5–15% weight loss over a year or more in people with type 2 diabetes, with the largest losses on higher doses of tirzepatide. Many of them also protect the heart and kidneys. They are covered in Chapter 8. They are not a substitute for healthy habits; muscle loss is a risk if protein intake and strength training are low.
- **Metabolic surgery.** For people with a BMI of 35 or more (sometimes lower) whose diabetes is hard to control, surgery such as a gastric bypass leads to remission in many cases. It is a major decision with lifelong follow-up.

## Keeping it off

Most people can lose weight. Keeping it off is harder, because the body fights back with increased hunger and slightly lower energy use. People who maintain weight loss tend to share these habits:

- They weigh themselves at least weekly and act early if the number rises by 2 kg.
- They are physically active for an hour or more most days, much of it walking.
- They eat breakfast and keep a fairly consistent eating pattern on weekdays and weekends.
- They keep tempting foods out of the house.
- They plan for setbacks: holidays, illness, stressful periods. A lapse is not a relapse.

> **What to do**
>
> - Set a first goal of losing 5% of your weight over 3–6 months. Write down your start weight and target.
> - If you are within 6 years of diagnosis, ask your doctor whether a structured remission programme is right for you.
> - Do not start a very low-calorie or very low-carbohydrate diet without your doctor adjusting your medicines first.
> - Weigh yourself weekly, at the same time, and react early to gains.
