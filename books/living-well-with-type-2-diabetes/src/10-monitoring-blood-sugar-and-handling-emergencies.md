# Chapter 9. Monitoring Blood Sugar and Handling Emergencies

## Why monitor at home

HbA1c tells you your average over three months. Home monitoring tells you what happens day to day: which meals spike your blood sugar, how exercise and sleep affect it, and whether your medicines are working. It turns diabetes from a number at the doctor’s into feedback you can act on.

## Ways to monitor

**Finger-prick meters.** A small drop of blood from the fingertip is placed on a strip. Meters are cheap and accurate enough for daily use.

- Wash and dry your hands first; sugar on the skin gives false high readings.
- Use the side of the fingertip, which hurts less.
- Store strips as instructed and check their expiry date.

**Continuous glucose monitors (CGM).** A small sensor worn on the arm or abdomen measures glucose every few minutes for 10–15 days and sends readings to a phone or reader. The 2026 ADA Standards of Care strengthened recommendations for CGM, including for people on insulin and on other medicines that can cause low blood sugar. Even for people not on insulin, a CGM worn for 2 weeks can be a powerful learning tool. Ask your doctor whether a CGM is available and reimbursed for you.

## When to test

If you take Xigduo or other medicines that rarely cause low blood sugar, you usually do not need to test every day. A useful approach is **structured testing:**

- **Paired testing:** test before a meal and 2 hours after starting it. The difference shows the effect of that meal. A rise of more than 3 mmol/L (about 50 mg/dL) suggests the meal had too many fast carbohydrates.
- **A 3-day profile** every month or two: test before and 2 hours after each main meal, and at bedtime, for three days. Bring the results to your doctor.
- **Morning fasting tests** a few times a week to track the trend.
- **When you feel unwell** or have symptoms of high or low blood sugar.
- **Before exercise and driving** if you take insulin or sulfonylureas.

Write down results with notes about food, activity, stress and sleep. Patterns matter more than single readings.

## Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia)

Low blood sugar is below **3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL)**. It is most common with insulin and sulfonylureas. Metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors alone rarely cause it, but it can happen with heavy exercise, alcohol, missed meals, or when other medicines are added.

**Symptoms:** shaking, sweating, fast heartbeat, hunger, anxiety, irritability, tingling lips, pale skin, blurred vision, confusion, difficulty concentrating, unusual behaviour. At night: nightmares, sweating, or waking with a headache.

**What to do – the “rule of 15”:**

1.  **Take 15–20 g of fast-acting sugar:** 4–5 glucose tablets, 150–200 mL of regular (not diet) juice or cola, or 3–4 teaspoons of sugar dissolved in water.
2.  **Wait 15 minutes** and test again.
3.  **If still below 3.9 mmol/L,** repeat.
4.  **Once above 3.9 mmol/L,** eat a snack with carbohydrate and protein if your next meal is more than an hour away.

Do not use chocolate or other fatty foods to treat a low; the fat slows sugar absorption.

**Severe hypoglycaemia,** when a person is confused, cannot swallow or is unconscious, is an emergency. Do not give food or drink by mouth. Call 112. If glucagon has been prescribed, a trained person can give it.

**If you drive** and take insulin or sulfonylureas: test before driving, do not drive below 5 mmol/L (90 mg/dL), and keep fast sugar in the car. If you have a low while driving, stop safely, take sugar, and wait at least 45 minutes after recovery before driving again.

## High blood sugar (hyperglycaemia)

High blood sugar is common during illness, stress, after steroid medicines, or when doses are missed.

**Symptoms:** thirst, frequent urination, tiredness, blurred vision, headache, and infections that heal slowly.

**What to do:**

- Drink water.
- Check whether you missed a dose or ate more carbohydrate than usual.
- Go for a walk if you feel well.
- If readings stay above 13–15 mmol/L (235–270 mg/dL) for more than a day, or you feel unwell, contact your doctor.

## Medical emergencies

**Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).** When the body lacks insulin action, it breaks down fat into ketones, which make the blood acidic. With SGLT2 inhibitors such as dapagliflozin, DKA can happen with only slightly raised blood sugar.

- **Symptoms:** nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, extreme tiredness, deep or fast breathing, fruity-smelling breath, confusion.
- **Action:** seek emergency care immediately. Tell staff you take an SGLT2 inhibitor. If you have ketone strips, a blood ketone level above 1.5 mmol/L is a warning, and above 3.0 mmol/L is an emergency.

**Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS).** Very high blood sugar, often above 30 mmol/L (540 mg/dL), with severe dehydration. It develops over days, often during infection, especially in older people.

- **Symptoms:** extreme thirst, very frequent urination, drowsiness, confusion.
- **Action:** emergency care.

**Heart attack and stroke.** People with diabetes sometimes have “silent” heart attacks with unusual symptoms because of nerve damage.

- **Heart attack symptoms:** chest pain, pressure or tightness, pain spreading to the arm, jaw or back, breathlessness, sweating, nausea, or sudden unexplained tiredness.
- **Stroke symptoms (FAST):** Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty – Time to call 112.

> **What to do**
>
> - Ask your doctor how often you should test, and whether a CGM is available to you.
> - Do paired testing around your usual meals to learn which ones spike you.
> - Learn the rule of 15 for low blood sugar, and keep fast sugar with you if you take insulin or sulfonylureas.
> - Learn the warning signs of ketoacidosis, heart attack and stroke. Call 112 without delay.
