# Chapter 5. Eating in Real Life

Knowing what to eat is the easy part. This chapter is about making it work at home, at work, at restaurants and at family celebrations.

## A sample day

Here is an example of a day of eating that fits the principles in Chapter 4. Adjust portions to your own needs.

**Breakfast (around 7–8 a.m.)**

- Oats cooked in water or milk, with a handful of berries, a tablespoon of ground flaxseed and a few walnuts.
- Or: two eggs with tomatoes, cucumber, a slice of rye bread and a little white cheese.
- Coffee or tea without sugar. Take your morning tablet with this meal if prescribed.

**Lunch (around 12–1 p.m.)**

- A large shopska salad with less cheese and a tablespoon of olive oil.
- Bean soup (bob chorba), lentil stew or grilled fish with vegetables.
- One slice of wholemeal bread.
- A 10–15 minute walk afterwards.

**Afternoon snack (if hungry)**

- Plain yogurt or kefir.
- Or: an apple with a handful of almonds.

**Dinner (around 6–7 p.m.)**

- Grilled chicken, turkey or fish, or an omelette.
- Roasted or steamed vegetables, or a salad.
- A small portion of bulgur, potatoes or rice, eaten last.
- A 10–15 minute walk afterwards. Take your evening tablet with this meal if prescribed.

## Smart swaps

| Instead of | Try |
|------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| White bread | Wholemeal, rye or seeded bread, in smaller amounts |
| Banitsa or pastries for breakfast | Oats, eggs, or yogurt with nuts |
| Fruit juice | Whole fruit, or water with lemon |
| Sweetened yogurt | Plain yogurt with berries |
| Sausages, lukanka, salami | Chicken, fish, eggs, beans |
| Frying in sunflower oil | Baking, grilling, or cooking with olive oil |
| White rice | Bulgur, buckwheat, brown rice, lentils |
| Sweets after dinner | A few nuts, berries, or a square of dark chocolate (70%+) |
| Crisps and salty snacks | Raw vegetables with yogurt dip, or a handful of nuts |
| Sugary coffee drinks | Coffee with milk, no sugar |

## Homemade soup

Homemade chicken, beef or vegetable soup is a good choice with diabetes. It is filling, low in calories, and brings protein and vegetables together. A few things decide how healthy a given soup is:

- **Salt is the main risk.** Stock cubes, packet soups and ready-made broths are very salty. Make the broth yourself, add salt at the end, and season with herbs, lemon, pepper or garlic.
- **Remove the fat.** Skim it while the soup cooks, or chill the broth and lift off the fat layer. Use skinless chicken and lean beef, such as shin or shoulder.
- **Add plenty of vegetables:** carrots, celery, onion, peppers, courgettes, cabbage and greens.
- **Go easy on the starchy parts.** Noodles, rice and potatoes raise blood sugar. Use a little, or use barley, lentils, chickpeas or beans instead.
- **Zastroyka is fine.** The egg-and-yogurt thickening adds protein and little fat, and is better than thickening with flour.
- **Eat the meat too.** Most of the protein is in the meat, not the broth, so a bowl with meat and vegetables can be a full meal.
- **Ignore “bone broth” claims.** There is no good evidence that it does anything special for joints, skin or the gut beyond being a nutritious soup.

A bowl of soup eaten before your bread or other carbohydrates also fits the “vegetables and protein first” rule from Chapter 4.

## Shopping and reading labels

- **Shop with a list,** and not when you are hungry.
- **Read the nutrition table.** Look at “carbohydrates, of which sugars”, “fibre” and “salt” per 100 g. As a rough guide, more than 22.5 g of sugar per 100 g is high and less than 5 g is low; more than 1.5 g of salt per 100 g is high.
- **Be wary of “diabetic” products.** Special diabetic sweets and biscuits are often high in fat and calories, and sweetened with sugar alcohols that can upset the stomach. They are not necessary.
- **Keep healthy foods visible** and tempting foods out of the house.

## Eating out and at work

- **Choose grilled, baked or stewed dishes** over fried ones.
- **Start with a salad or soup.**
- **Ask for sauces and dressings on the side.**
- **Skip the bread basket,** or take one slice.
- **Share desserts,** or skip them.
- **At work,** bring your own lunch when you can, and keep nuts or fruit in your desk instead of buying snacks.

## Family celebrations and holidays

Bulgarian celebrations often include large meals, homemade pastries and rakia. You do not have to avoid them.

- Eat a normal meal earlier in the day instead of “saving up” calories, which leads to overeating.
- Fill your plate with salads and grilled meats first.
- Take a small piece of the dessert or banitsa you really want, and enjoy it slowly.
- Walk after the meal, even for 10 minutes.
- Do not skip your tablets.

## Alcohol

Alcohol affects diabetes in several ways:

- It is high in calories (7 kcal per gram) and increases appetite.
- It is one of the strongest drivers of belly fat and fatty liver.
- It raises blood pressure and triglycerides.
- It can cause low blood sugar, sometimes hours later, if you take insulin or sulfonylureas.
- With SGLT2 inhibitors such as dapagliflozin, heavy drinking increases the risk of ketoacidosis.

If you drink, keep it moderate: no more than about 1–2 standard drinks a day for men and 1 for women, with several alcohol-free days each week. A standard drink is roughly 330 mL of beer, 125 mL of wine or 30–40 mL of rakia. Less is better. Drink with food, never on an empty stomach, and avoid sweet cocktails and liqueurs.

## Fruit

Whole fruit is part of a healthy diet for people with diabetes. Its fibre, vitamins and polyphenols are valuable. Two to three portions a day is a good guide; a portion is one medium apple or orange, two kiwis, or a cup of berries.

- **Lower-sugar choices:** berries, apples, pears, oranges, grapefruit, kiwi, plums, cherries.
- **Higher-sugar choices,** in smaller portions: grapes, ripe bananas, persimmons, mango, figs, watermelon.
- **Avoid:** fruit juice and large amounts of dried fruit.

Eating fruit with yogurt or nuts slows the sugar rise. A fruit-only dinner is acceptable occasionally, but a dinner with protein and vegetables is a better habit, especially if you take metformin and dapagliflozin.

## Coping with hunger and cravings

- **Eat enough protein and fibre** at each meal.
- **Drink a glass of water** first; thirst is often mistaken for hunger.
- **Wait 15 minutes** before giving in to a craving; many pass.
- **Get enough sleep.** Short sleep increases hunger hormones.
- **Notice emotional eating.** If you eat when stressed, bored or tired, find another response: a walk, a call, a shower.

> **What to do**
>
> - Plan your meals for the week, and shop with a list.
> - Choose 3 swaps from the table and make them this week.
> - Limit alcohol to a small amount with food, with several alcohol-free days a week.
> - Keep 2–3 portions of whole fruit a day, mostly lower-sugar fruits.
