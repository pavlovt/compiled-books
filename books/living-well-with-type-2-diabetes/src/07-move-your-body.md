# Chapter 6. Move Your Body

## Why exercise is medicine

Muscles are the largest consumer of glucose in the body. When they contract, they pull glucose out of the blood through a pathway that partly bypasses insulin. This is why a single walk lowers blood sugar within minutes, and why regular exercise improves insulin sensitivity for 24–72 hours after each session.

Regular physical activity in type 2 diabetes:

- Lowers HbA1c by about 0.5–0.7 percentage points on average, similar to adding a medicine.
- Lowers blood pressure and triglycerides, and raises HDL cholesterol.
- Reduces visceral fat and liver fat, even without much weight loss.
- Builds and preserves muscle, which matters more every year after 50.
- Improves sleep, mood and energy.
- Is the strongest predictor of keeping lost weight off.

## The weekly targets

International guidelines, including the American Diabetes Association Standards of Care, recommend for adults with type 2 diabetes:

1.  **At least 150 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity,** spread over at least 3 days, with no more than 2 days in a row without activity. Moderate means you can talk but not sing: brisk walking, cycling, swimming, or an exercise bike.
2.  **Resistance (strength) training 2–3 times a week** on non-consecutive days.
3.  **Breaking up sitting** every 30 minutes with a few minutes of light movement.
4.  **Flexibility and balance exercises** 2–3 times a week, especially after 50.

More is better: 200–300 minutes a week of activity helps with weight loss and maintenance.

## Walking after meals

The simplest high-impact habit is a 10–15 minute walk after meals, starting within 30 minutes of finishing. In a crossover study of people with type 2 diabetes, three 10-minute walks after meals lowered post-meal blood sugar by about 12% compared with one 30-minute walk at another time of day. After dinner, the reduction was about 22%. The muscles take up glucose just as it arrives from the meal.

If you cannot walk outside, walk around the house, climb stairs, or use an exercise bike at an easy pace.

## When to exercise

The most important thing is to exercise at a time you can keep to. For blood sugar, the best moments are right after meals, especially after dinner, which is usually the highest point of the day.

- **Fit longer workouts wherever they suit you.** Brisk walking, cycling or strength training can go in the morning, afternoon or early evening. Late-afternoon or evening sessions may give slightly better blood sugar control, but the difference is small compared with simply exercising regularly.
- **Finish hard exercise 2–3 hours before bed,** because late, intense workouts can disturb sleep. A gentle walk after dinner is fine.
- **Avoid hard exercise on an empty stomach** until you know how your body responds.

A simple routine is a short walk after lunch and dinner every day, plus 2–3 strength sessions a week at whatever time is convenient.

## Strength training

Strength training builds muscle, which acts as a “glucose sink”. It improves insulin sensitivity, protects bones and joints, and prevents the loss of muscle and strength that comes with age. Combining strength and aerobic training lowers HbA1c more than either alone.

A basic home programme with dumbbells covers the main movements:

| Movement | Example exercise | Sets × repetitions |
|----|----|----|
| Squat | Goblet squat, or sit-to-stand from a chair | 2–3 × 10–15 |
| Hinge | Romanian deadlift | 2–3 × 10–12 |
| Pull | One-arm dumbbell row | 2–3 × 10–12 per arm |
| Push | Floor press or push-ups | 2–3 × 10–12 |
| Overhead push | Seated shoulder press | 2 × 10–12 |
| Single leg | Supported lunges or step-ups | 2 × 8–10 per leg |
| Core | Plank or dead bug | 2–3 × 20–40 seconds |

**Key principles:**

- **Effort:** choose a weight where the last 2–3 repetitions are hard, with good form.
- **Progression:** when you can do the top number of repetitions in all sets easily, add 1–2 kg or make the exercise harder.
- **Breathing:** breathe out as you lift and in as you lower. Do not hold your breath, as this raises blood pressure.
- **Rest:** 60–90 seconds between sets.
- **Start gently:** in the first two weeks, do one set of each exercise, twice a week.

## Higher-intensity exercise

Once you have a base of fitness, short bursts of higher intensity improve fitness and insulin sensitivity efficiently. On an exercise bike, for example: warm up for 5 minutes, then do 30 seconds hard and 90 seconds easy, repeated 4–6 times, then cool down. “Hard” is relative: for a beginner, a brisk walk uphill may be enough.

Talk to your doctor before starting high-intensity exercise if you have heart disease, chest pain, uncontrolled blood pressure, advanced eye disease or are new to exercise after long inactivity.

## Exercise safety with diabetes

- **Low blood sugar.** Metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors alone rarely cause low blood sugar during exercise. If you take insulin or sulfonylureas (gliclazide, glimepiride), check your blood sugar before exercise and carry fast sugar such as glucose tablets or juice.
- **Fluids.** SGLT2 inhibitors increase fluid loss through urine. Drink water before, during and after exercise, especially in hot weather.
- **Feet.** Wear well-fitting shoes and clean socks. Check your feet after exercise, especially if you have numbness.
- **Eyes.** If you have moderate or severe diabetic retinopathy, avoid very heavy lifting, straining and head-down positions; ask your eye doctor what is safe.
- **Blood pressure.** If your blood pressure is above 180/110 mmHg, do not exercise until it is treated.
- **Warning signs.** Stop and seek medical help if you have chest pain or pressure, severe breathlessness, dizziness, fainting, or an irregular heartbeat.

## Fitting it in

- **Make it a routine:** same days and times each week.
- **Stack habits:** walk after lunch, do strength training while dinner cooks.
- **Count everything:** stairs, cycling to work, gardening, playing with grandchildren.
- **Use a step counter.** If you currently walk 4,000 steps a day, aim for 6,000, then 8,000. Benefits continue to rise up to about 8,000–10,000 steps a day.
- **Have a bad-weather plan:** an exercise bike, a home routine, or a mall walk.
- **Use an app if it helps.** Your phone’s built-in health app (Samsung Health, Google Fit or Apple Health) counts steps and active minutes, which is enough to track after-meal walks and the 150-minute target. For home strength training, Nike Training Club has free beginner and low-impact workouts you can filter by length; FitOn suits people who prefer following a class. Hevy is a free log for your own routine if you want to track sets and progress. Start at the easiest level and build up gradually. Apps and their free features change, so pick one you actually use.

> **What to do**
>
> - Walk for 10–15 minutes after your two largest meals every day.
> - Do strength training 2–3 times a week on non-consecutive days.
> - Build up to at least 150 minutes a week of brisk walking, cycling or similar.
> - Stand up and move for 2–3 minutes every 30 minutes of sitting.
> - Drink enough water, especially on exercise days.
