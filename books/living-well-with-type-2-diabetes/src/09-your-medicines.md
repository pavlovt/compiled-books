# Chapter 8. Your Medicines

## Why medicines matter

Lifestyle is the foundation of diabetes care, but most people with type 2 diabetes also need medicines, and many need more over time. Needing medicine is not a failure. Modern diabetes medicines do more than lower blood sugar: several of them protect the heart and kidneys and help with weight loss.

The 2026 American Diabetes Association Standards of Care and European guidelines recommend choosing medicines based on your other conditions, not only on your HbA1c. For people with heart disease, heart failure or chronic kidney disease, an SGLT2 inhibitor or a GLP-1 receptor agonist with proven benefit is recommended regardless of blood sugar level.

## The main groups of diabetes medicines

| Group | Examples | How it works | Main benefits | Main cautions |
|---------------|---------------|---------------|---------------|---------------|
| Biguanide | Metformin | Reduces glucose release by the liver; improves insulin sensitivity | Effective, cheap, weight-neutral, very low risk of low blood sugar | Stomach upset; vitamin B12 deficiency over years; stop temporarily when dehydrated or before some scans |
| SGLT2 inhibitors | Dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, canagliflozin | Make the kidneys pass extra glucose into the urine | Protect the heart and kidneys; reduce heart failure; modest weight and blood pressure loss | Genital yeast infections; dehydration; rare ketoacidosis, even with normal sugar |
| GLP-1 receptor agonists | Semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide | Boost insulin after meals, slow stomach emptying, reduce appetite | Strong HbA1c and weight reduction; several protect the heart and kidneys | Nausea at first; expensive; injections for most |
| GIP/GLP-1 agonist | Tirzepatide | Acts on two gut hormone receptors | Largest HbA1c and weight reductions | Nausea; cost |
| DPP-4 inhibitors | Sitagliptin, linagliptin, vildagliptin | Prolong the action of your own GLP-1 | Well tolerated, weight-neutral | Modest effect |
| Sulfonylureas | Gliclazide, glimepiride | Force the pancreas to release more insulin | Cheap and effective | Low blood sugar; weight gain |
| Pioglitazone | Pioglitazone | Improves insulin sensitivity | Helps fatty liver | Weight gain, fluid retention, fractures |
| Insulin | Basal and mealtime insulins | Replaces missing insulin | Most powerful; no maximum dose | Low blood sugar; weight gain; needs monitoring |

## Xigduo: dapagliflozin plus metformin

Xigduo combines two medicines in one tablet: **dapagliflozin** (an SGLT2 inhibitor) and **metformin**. In Europe it is usually taken twice a day, with meals.

**How to take it:**

- **Take it with meals,** usually breakfast and dinner. Food reduces the stomach upset caused by metformin.
- **Swallow it whole.**
- **Do not change the timing or skip doses** without asking your doctor.
- **If you miss a dose,** take it with your next meal. Do not take two doses at once.

**What it does well:**

- Lowers HbA1c, usually by about 1 percentage point or more compared with no treatment.
- Rarely causes low blood sugar on its own.
- Dapagliflozin protects the kidneys and reduces hospital admissions for heart failure.
- Often leads to modest weight loss of 2–3 kg.

**Common side effects and what to do:**

- **Stomach upset, nausea or diarrhoea,** mostly from metformin and usually in the first weeks. Taking it with food helps. Tell your doctor if it persists.
- **More frequent urination and thirst,** because glucose is passed in the urine. Drink enough water.
- **Genital yeast infections** (itching, redness, discharge). Good hygiene helps. Infections are usually easily treated; tell your doctor.
- **Urinary tract infections.**

**Rare but serious problems:**

- **Ketoacidosis.** Dapagliflozin can rarely cause diabetic ketoacidosis, sometimes with normal or only slightly raised blood sugar (“euglycaemic ketoacidosis”). Risk is higher during illness, vomiting, dehydration, surgery, fasting, very low-carbohydrate diets, and heavy alcohol use. **Get urgent medical help** for nausea, vomiting, stomach pain, unusual tiredness, fast or deep breathing, or confusion, and tell the doctors you take an SGLT2 inhibitor.
- **Lactic acidosis.** Metformin can very rarely cause lactic acidosis, mainly in people with severe kidney, liver or heart failure, or during severe dehydration. The symptoms are similar: vomiting, stomach pain, muscle cramps, breathlessness, severe weakness.
- **Fournier’s gangrene.** A very rare but serious infection of the genital area. Seek help urgently for pain, tenderness, redness or swelling in the genital or perineal area with fever or feeling unwell.

**Before surgery or scans:**

- Tell your doctors and the surgical team that you take Xigduo.
- Dapagliflozin is usually stopped at least 3 days before planned surgery.
- Metformin may need to be paused around surgery and around some scans that use iodine contrast dye, depending on kidney function.

**Long-term:**

- Ask for a **vitamin B12 test** periodically, especially if you have numbness, tingling or tiredness.
- Your doctor will check **kidney function** regularly; doses may need adjustment if it falls.

## Sick-day rules

When you are ill, especially with vomiting, diarrhoea, fever, or when you cannot eat or drink normally, some medicines become risky because of dehydration. A common rule of thumb in Europe is known by the letters **SADMANS**. These medicines are often paused temporarily on sick days:

- **S**ulfonylureas
- **A**CE inhibitors
- **D**iuretics
- **M**etformin
- **A**ngiotensin receptor blockers
- **N**on-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (such as ibuprofen)
- **S**GLT2 inhibitors (such as dapagliflozin)

**On a sick day:**

1.  **Drink plenty of fluids:** at least a glass every hour while awake.
2.  **Try to eat small amounts of carbohydrate** regularly, such as toast, crackers, soup or fruit, even if you are not hungry.
3.  **Check your blood sugar more often** if you have a meter.
4.  **Ask your doctor in advance** which of your medicines to pause on sick days, and when to restart. Usually you restart once you have been eating and drinking normally for 24–48 hours.
5.  **Get medical help** if you cannot keep fluids down, vomit for more than a few hours, have signs of ketoacidosis, or your blood sugar stays above 15 mmol/L (270 mg/dL).

## Other medicines you are likely to need

Most people with type 2 diabetes also take medicines to protect the heart and kidneys:

- **Statins** to lower LDL cholesterol. Recommended for most adults with diabetes aged 40 and over, and for younger people with risk factors.
- **Blood pressure medicines,** especially ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers when there is protein in the urine.
- **Finerenone,** for some people with diabetic kidney disease.
- **Aspirin,** for people who already have heart or artery disease. For people without heart disease, aspirin is not routinely recommended, because the bleeding risk often outweighs the benefit.

These are covered further in Chapter 10.

## Getting the most from your medicines

- **Keep an up-to-date list** of all your medicines and doses, including supplements, and carry it with you.
- **Use a pill organiser** or phone reminders.
- **Do not stop a medicine because you feel well.** High blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol usually cause no symptoms.
- **Report side effects.** There is usually an alternative.
- **Ask your pharmacist** about interactions before taking any new medicine or supplement, including over-the-counter painkillers.
- **Review your medicines with your doctor** at least once a year.

> **What to do**
>
> - Take Xigduo with breakfast and dinner, as prescribed. Do not skip doses or change the timing on your own.
> - Learn the warning signs of ketoacidosis, and seek help urgently if they appear.
> - Ask your doctor for your personal sick-day rules: which medicines to pause and when to restart.
> - Tell every doctor, dentist and surgeon that you take an SGLT2 inhibitor and metformin.
> - Ask whether you need a statin and whether a GLP-1 medicine could help you.
