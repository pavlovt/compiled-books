# Chapter 2. Know Your Numbers

You cannot manage what you do not measure. This chapter lists the numbers that matter, what each one means, and the usual targets. Your doctor may set different targets for you based on your age, other conditions and medicines. Write your own targets in the table at the end of this chapter.

## HbA1c: your three-month average

HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) measures how much glucose is stuck to your red blood cells. Because red blood cells live about three months, HbA1c reflects your average blood sugar over the last 2–3 months. It is the main number your doctor uses to judge diabetes control.

| HbA1c (%) | HbA1c (mmol/mol) | Approximate average glucose |
|-----------|------------------|-----------------------------|
| 6.0       | 42               | 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL)      |
| 6.5       | 48               | 7.8 mmol/L (140 mg/dL)      |
| 7.0       | 53               | 8.6 mmol/L (154 mg/dL)      |
| 8.0       | 64               | 10.2 mmol/L (183 mg/dL)     |
| 9.0       | 75               | 11.8 mmol/L (212 mg/dL)     |

**Usual target:** below 7% (53 mmol/mol) for most adults. A lower target, such as below 6.5%, may suit people early in the disease if it can be reached safely without low blood sugar. A higher target, such as below 8%, may suit older people or those with other serious illnesses.

Every 1 percentage point reduction in HbA1c lowers the risk of eye, kidney and nerve complications substantially. Getting from 9% to 8% is worth almost as much as getting from 8% to 7%, so progress at any level counts.

**How often:** every 3 months when treatment is changing or above target; every 6 months when stable and at target.

## Daily blood glucose

If you check your blood sugar with a meter or a continuous glucose monitor (CGM), these are the usual targets for adults:

| Time                                | Target                        |
|-------------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Before meals (fasting)              | 4.4–7.2 mmol/L (80–130 mg/dL) |
| 1–2 hours after the start of a meal | Below 10.0 mmol/L (180 mg/dL) |
| Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia)     | Below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL)   |
| Serious low blood sugar             | Below 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL)   |

With a CGM, the main number is **time in range**: the share of the day spent between 3.9 and 10.0 mmol/L (70–180 mg/dL). The usual goal is more than 70% of the time in range, less than 4% below 3.9 mmol/L, and less than 1% below 3.0 mmol/L. Chapter 9 explains when and how to test.

## Blood pressure

High blood pressure is common in type 2 diabetes and roughly doubles the damage to the heart, kidneys and eyes.

**Usual target:** below 130/80 mmHg, if it can be reached safely.

**How often:** at every doctor’s visit, and ideally at home. Home measurement is described in Chapter 10.

## Cholesterol and blood fats

The most important blood fat is **LDL cholesterol**, the “bad” cholesterol that builds up in artery walls.

European guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (updated in 2025, with targets unchanged from 2019) place most people with type 2 diabetes in the high or very high cardiovascular risk group:

| Risk group | Typical situation | LDL target |
|------------------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| High risk | Diabetes without organ damage, but with diabetes for 10 years or more, or with another risk factor | Below 1.8 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) |
| Very high risk | Diabetes with organ damage (kidney, eye or nerve damage), or with 3 or more major risk factors, or with established heart or artery disease | Below 1.4 mmol/L (55 mg/dL) |

Other numbers on the lipid panel are **HDL cholesterol** (higher is better), **triglycerides** (ideally below 1.7 mmol/L, or 150 mg/dL) and **non-HDL cholesterol**.

**How often:** at diagnosis, then at least once a year, and 4–12 weeks after starting or changing cholesterol medicine.

## Kidney tests

Two tests together show how your kidneys are doing:

- **eGFR** (estimated glomerular filtration rate), from a blood test of creatinine. It estimates how well your kidneys filter. Above 90 mL/min/1.73 m² is normal; below 60 means chronic kidney disease.
- **Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR),** from a urine sample. It detects small amounts of protein leaking into the urine, which is the earliest sign of kidney damage. Normal is below 3 mg/mmol (30 mg/g).

**How often:** at least once a year, more often if either is abnormal. Ask specifically for the urine test: it is often skipped, yet it catches damage years earlier than eGFR.

## Weight and waist

- **Weight:** weigh yourself at the same time of day, ideally in the morning after using the toilet, once a week or daily if you prefer. Look at the trend, not single days.
- **Waist:** measure at the level of the navel, standing, after breathing out. For European men, a waist above 94 cm means increased risk and above 102 cm means substantially increased risk. For women, the figures are 80 cm and 88 cm. Waist size tracks visceral fat better than weight does.
- **Body mass index (BMI):** weight in kg divided by height in metres squared. 25–29.9 is overweight; 30 or more is obesity. BMI is a rough guide; waist size adds important information.

## Other tests worth knowing

- **Liver tests (ALT, AST)** and a **fatty liver risk score** called FIB-4, which your doctor calculates from your age, liver enzymes and platelet count. Fatty liver disease is very common in type 2 diabetes.
- **Vitamin B12,** especially if you take metformin for years. Metformin can reduce B12 absorption.
- **Thyroid function,** if you have symptoms.
- **Potassium and sodium,** if you take blood pressure medicines that affect them.
