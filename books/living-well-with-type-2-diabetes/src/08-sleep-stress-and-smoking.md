# Chapter 7. Sleep, Stress and Smoking

## Sleep

Sleep is not a luxury for people with diabetes. Short or poor sleep:

- Raises insulin resistance within days. In laboratory studies, healthy adults restricted to about 4–5 hours a night became markedly less sensitive to insulin within a week.
- Increases hunger hormones and cravings for sugary, fatty foods.
- In one controlled study, two weeks of sleep restriction led people to eat about 300 extra calories a day and increased fat inside the abdomen.
- Raises blood pressure and stress hormones.

People with type 2 diabetes who sleep less than 6 hours, or more than 9, tend to have higher HbA1c.

**Targets:** 7–8 hours a night, on a regular schedule.

**Habits that help:**

1.  **Keep the same wake-up time** every day, including weekends.
2.  **Get daylight in the morning,** ideally outdoors within an hour of waking.
3.  **Finish dinner 3–4 hours before bed,** and avoid alcohol late in the evening. Alcohol helps you fall asleep but worsens sleep quality.
4.  **Limit caffeine after 2 p.m.**
5.  **Keep the bedroom cool, dark and quiet.**
6.  **Put screens away** 30–60 minutes before bed.
7.  **If you cannot sleep** after 20 minutes, get up, do something calm in dim light, and return when sleepy.

### Sleep apnoea

Obstructive sleep apnoea is very common in people with type 2 diabetes, especially men with a large neck or waist. The airway collapses repeatedly during sleep, causing drops in oxygen and broken sleep. It worsens blood sugar and blood pressure and raises the risk of heart disease and stroke.

**Warning signs:** loud snoring, pauses in breathing noticed by a partner, waking with a choking feeling, morning headaches, and heavy daytime sleepiness. If you have these, ask your doctor about a sleep study. Treatment with a CPAP machine, and weight loss, can transform sleep and energy.

## Stress and emotional health

Chronic stress raises cortisol and adrenaline, which push blood sugar up and encourage fat storage around the waist. Stress also makes it harder to eat well, exercise and sleep.

Living with diabetes is itself stressful. Many people experience **diabetes distress**: feeling overwhelmed by the daily demands of managing the condition, worrying about complications, or feeling guilty about numbers. Depression is also about twice as common in people with diabetes as in the general population, and it makes diabetes harder to manage.

**Practical tools:**

- **Physical activity** is one of the most effective stress relievers.
- **Breathing exercises:** breathe in slowly for 4 seconds and out for 6 seconds, for 5 minutes.
- **Time outdoors,** especially walking in green spaces.
- **Social contact:** time with family and friends, or a diabetes support group.
- **Limit news and social media** if they raise your stress.
- **Break goals into small steps,** and notice progress.
- **Talk about it.** If you feel low most days for more than two weeks, lose interest in things you used to enjoy, or feel hopeless, tell your doctor. Depression is treatable, and treating it improves diabetes control.

## Smoking

Smoking is the single most harmful habit for a person with diabetes. Diabetes and smoking both damage blood vessels, and together they multiply the risk of heart attack, stroke, amputation, kidney failure and sight loss. Smoking also increases insulin resistance.

Stopping is the best thing a smoker with diabetes can do for their health. The benefits start within weeks: blood pressure and circulation improve, and the risk of heart attack begins to fall within a year.

**What helps:**

- **Set a quit date** and tell people.
- **Use nicotine replacement** (patches, gum, lozenges) or prescription medicines such as varenicline or cytisine. These roughly double the chances of quitting.
- **Get support:** from your doctor, a pharmacist or a quit programme.
- **Expect some weight gain** and plan for it with diet and exercise. The health gain from quitting far outweighs a few kilograms.

E-cigarettes and heated tobacco are less harmful than smoking but not harmless. If you use them to quit, the goal should be to stop them too.

> **What to do**
>
> - Aim for 7–8 hours of sleep on a regular schedule, with morning daylight.
> - If you snore loudly or feel sleepy during the day, ask your doctor about sleep apnoea.
> - Choose one daily stress-relief habit, such as a walk or breathing exercise.
> - Tell your doctor if you feel low or overwhelmed for more than two weeks.
> - If you smoke, make a plan to stop, and ask for medicine and support.
