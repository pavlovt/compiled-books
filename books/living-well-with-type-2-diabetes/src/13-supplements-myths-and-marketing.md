# Chapter 12. Supplements, Myths and Marketing

## The truth about supplements

Diabetes is a huge market for supplements, “natural cures” and special products. Most have little or no good evidence behind them. Some can be harmful or interact with your medicines. The American Diabetes Association Standards of Care state that there is no clear evidence that dietary supplements, vitamins, minerals, herbs or spices improve outcomes in people with diabetes who do not have a deficiency.

The general rule: **supplements correct deficiencies; they do not treat diabetes.**

## Supplements worth considering only for a clear reason

| Supplement | When it may make sense | Notes |
|------------------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| Vitamin B12 | Proven deficiency, often from long-term metformin | Ask for a blood test; take only if low or borderline with symptoms |
| Vitamin D | Proven deficiency, little sun exposure, osteoporosis | Common in winter in Bulgaria; the large VITAL and D2d trials did not show a meaningful effect on diabetes outcomes in people without deficiency |
| Omega-3 (fish oil) | High triglycerides, if your doctor recommends it | Eating oily fish twice a week is better for most people; high-dose prescription EPA is a separate medical treatment |
| Fibre supplements (psyllium) | When you cannot eat enough fibre from food | Can help blood sugar and LDL; drink plenty of water |
| Calcium | Low dietary intake and bone health concerns | Food sources are preferred |

## Supplements to be cautious about

- **Berberine.** It does lower blood sugar modestly, but it acts somewhat like metformin and can add to the effect of your medicines, cause stomach upset, and interact with other drugs. Product quality varies widely. Do not take it with Xigduo unless your doctor approves.
- **Cinnamon capsules.** Results are inconsistent. Some cassia cinnamon contains coumarin, which can harm the liver in high doses. Cinnamon in food is fine.
- **Chromium, magnesium, zinc and alpha-lipoic acid.** Evidence is mixed or weak in people without deficiency. Alpha-lipoic acid is sometimes used for painful neuropathy; discuss it with your doctor.
- **Green tea extract.** High-dose capsules have been linked to serious liver injury. Drinking green tea is safe and may be beneficial; concentrated extracts are not the same.
- **Bitter melon, fenugreek, gymnema, ginseng.** Small or poor-quality studies; possible interactions and low blood sugar when combined with medicines.
- **“Detox” products, “sugar blockers” and miracle cures** sold online or on social media. Avoid them. Some products have been found to contain hidden prescription drugs.

Always tell your doctor and pharmacist about any supplement, herbal tea or “natural” product you take.

## Common myths

**“Eating sugar causes diabetes.”** Type 2 diabetes is caused by a mix of genes, excess body fat (especially visceral and liver fat), inactivity and age. Sugary drinks raise risk mainly by adding calories and weight.

**“People with diabetes must never eat sugar or fruit.”** Small amounts of sugar within a healthy meal pattern are acceptable. Whole fruit is beneficial. Juice and sugary drinks are the main things to avoid.

**“If I feel fine, my diabetes is fine.”** High blood sugar, blood pressure and cholesterol usually cause no symptoms while they cause damage. Only tests tell you.

**“Needing medicine means I have failed.”** Type 2 diabetes tends to progress. Medicines are tools that protect your organs. Many modern ones also protect the heart and kidneys.

**“Insulin causes blindness or amputations.”** Insulin does not cause complications. High blood sugar does. People sometimes start insulin late, when complications already exist, which creates the false link.

**“Diabetic products are healthier.”** Special “diabetic” sweets are often high in fat and calories and offer no benefit.

**“Once I am in remission, I am cured.”** Remission depends on keeping the weight off. Continue your check-ups, including eye and kidney tests, because past high blood sugar can still cause problems.

**“Carbohydrates must be cut to zero.”** Very low-carbohydrate diets can help some people lose weight, but they are not required. For people on SGLT2 inhibitors they can raise the risk of ketoacidosis and should only be done with medical supervision.

**“A YouTube doctor said…”** Some online health advice is good, but much is oversold. Be sceptical of claims about “secret” foods, dramatic results in 14 days, and anything that tells you to stop your medicines. Check advice with your doctor before acting on it.

## How to judge health claims

Ask these questions:

1.  **Was it tested in people** or only in cells or animals?
2.  **How many people, and for how long?** A 4-day study in 11 people is a hint, not proof.
3.  **Did it measure what matters?** Lower blood sugar after one meal is not the same as fewer heart attacks.
4.  **Who is selling it?** Be cautious when the person making the claim profits from it.
5.  **Does it fit with major guidelines?** If a claim contradicts the ADA, ESC or your national guidelines, ask why.

> **What to do**
>
> - Do not take supplements to treat diabetes. Take them only to correct a proven deficiency.
> - Ask for a B12 test if you have taken metformin for years.
> - Tell your doctor and pharmacist about every supplement or herbal product you use.
> - Be sceptical of online claims that promise fast, dramatic results.
